\title{Do Indian Companies Register on Auspicious Days?}
\author{Gaurav Sood}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Hindu almanacs mark some days as good for starting a business and some as bad. Using all \NDomestic{} domestic companies registered in India between October 2006 and January 2020, counted by registrar and day, I test three predictions written down before estimation. Registrations do not fall during Pitru Paksha, the fortnight widely avoided for new beginnings: the estimate is \POnePct\% (95\% CI \POneLo{} to \POneHi). Registrations do not track the daily lunar day (\tithi): the estimate is \PThreePct\% per step of a three-level auspiciousness index, with an interval that excludes effects of about one percent. Registrations do rise on named \muhurat{} days, by \PTwoPct\% (95\% CI \PTwoLo{} to \PTwoHi; Holm-adjusted $p = \PTwoHolm$), a rise that comes from Dhanteras and Gudi Padwa and not from Akshaya Tritiya. Registrations on the days around them fall slightly, so the rise may be moved from neighbouring days rather than added, though that comparison is imprecise; the rise also weakens in two of six pre-listed robustness checks. The panchang is computed from the ephemeris and matches a published almanac on every festival and lunar day checked within the sample years.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Indian businesses consult the \panchang, the Hindu almanac, to choose when to open a shop, sign a lease or launch a venture. Traders do \muhurat{} trading on Diwali evening, and car dealers and property registrars report steep drops during Pitru Paksha, the fortnight of rites for ancestors \citep{bs2014pitru}. If founders time company registrations the same way, the timing should be visible in the government registry, which records the date each company was incorporated.

I find little of it. Across \NDomestic{} registrations, Pitru Paksha leaves no mark and neither does the daily lunar calendar. The one prediction that holds is a rise of \PTwoPct\% on four named \muhurat{} days; in practice three, because Vijayadashami is a public holiday. The rise comes from two of the three: Dhanteras, the day before Diwali on which Hindus buy gold and begin ventures (+\DhanterasPct\%), and Gudi Padwa, the Hindu new year in western and southern India (+\GudiPct\%). Akshaya Tritiya, often called the most auspicious day of the year for new ventures, shows nothing (\AksPct\%).

Two features of the design matter for reading these numbers. First, the main threat is not belief but holidays. Many festivals fall on fixed lunar days and close the registrar's office, so a naive comparison finds that ``inauspicious'' days have fewer registrations because the office was shut. I remove every registrar-day that was a public holiday in that registrar's state or that registered almost nothing, and compare each day only with other days of the same week at the same registrar. Second, the three tests, their models and their power were written down and committed before any of them was run (\texttt{ms/pap.md}).

The paper adds to evidence that calendar beliefs move real decisions: births timed to the Chinese Dragon year \citep{mocan2017superstition}, prices clustered on lucky digits \citep{brown2008culture} and trading around unlucky days \citep{hirshleifer2018superstition}. Its contribution is a well-measured case where most of the predicted timing does not appear.

\section{The panchang}

The \panchang{} describes each day by five astronomical quantities. The two used most for choosing days are the \tithi{} and the \nakshatra. A \tithi{} is one of 30 equal steps of the Moon's angular distance from the Sun, about a day long; a \nakshatra{} is one of 27 sectors of the sky the Moon passes through. Manuals of electional astrology classify each value as auspicious, inauspicious or neutral for beginnings \citep{kane1974history}. Table~\ref{tab:sample} gives the shares of business days in each class.

Some days are named. Gudi Padwa (also Ugadi) opens the lunar year; Akshaya Tritiya is the third lunar day of the month Vaishakha; Dhanteras falls two days before Diwali; Vijayadashami ends the autumn Navratri festival. Pitru Paksha is the waning fortnight before Navratri, given to rites for ancestors and widely avoided for purchases and new ventures.

I compute the \panchang{} for every day from 2005 to 2026 with the Swiss Ephemeris \citep{swisseph2024} at sunrise in New Delhi, following the convention that the value prevailing at sunrise names the day. Named days need a further rule: each festival has a prescribed time of day, evening for Dhanteras and Diwali, afternoon for Vijayadashami and the Pitru Paksha rites, forenoon for Akshaya Tritiya, and falls on the day its lunar day covers that time, the later day when it covers it on two. Table~\ref{tab:validation} compares the result with two sources that share no code with mine: Drik Panchang's New Delhi almanac \citep{drikpanchang2024}, on which I tuned the time-of-day rules, and the public-holiday calendars of the \texttt{holidays} package, which I did not use for tuning. Within the sample years the two agree with my dates on every check but one, a Gudi Padwa on which Drik agrees with me and the package does not. After the sample the rules hold less well: \ValPkgOut{} of \ValPkgOutN{} festival dates in 2020--2025 match; each miss is off by one day. None falls in the estimation window.

\section{Data}

The Ministry of Corporate Affairs publishes a master file of companies on its register. I use the December 2020 snapshot, which lists \NCompanies{} companies registered from October 2006, when electronic filing became compulsory, to January 2020, when the snapshot ends. It includes companies later struck off but may miss companies dissolved or merged before 2020; the estimates assume that whether a company survived to the snapshot is unrelated to the lunar day it was registered on. The date in the file is the date the registrar approved the company, not the date the founder applied. Until March 2016 each of \NRocs{} regional registrars approved its own companies, often days or weeks after filing; from 23 March 2016 a Central Registration Centre approved them nationally, usually within a day or two.

The unit is a registrar on a business day: \NRocDays{} registrar-days across \NBusinessDays{} weekdays. The outcome is the number of domestic non-government companies approved; foreign subsidiaries (\NForeign) and government companies (\NGovt) serve as placebo populations whose timing is set by corporate headquarters or ministries.

Closures are the main threat. Registrars close on public holidays, and many Hindu holidays fall on fixed lunar days, so a closed office would look like a day founders avoided. I mark a registrar-day closed if it is a public holiday in the registrar's state (in the national calendar after March 2016) according to the \texttt{holidays} package, or if registrations fall below a tenth of that week's median at that registrar or nationally. Figure~\ref{fig:closures} shows that the two sources agree: \ShareClosedListedAgree\% of detected closures are listed holidays, and \ShareListedClosed\% of listed holidays show a closure. The rest are mostly Islamic holidays observed a day off the listed date and state festivals missing from the package. I drop \NRocDaysClosed{} closed registrar-days. I also drop \NDisruptedWeeks{} weeks in which national registrations fell below half their 17-week rolling median, chiefly April--May 2014, when the forms under the Companies Act 2013 took effect, and late March--April 2016, when the central registry took over (Figure~\ref{fig:series}). Akshaya Tritiya 2014 falls in the first of these.

\section{Design}

\paragraph{Three tests.} Each is a Poisson regression of a registrar's daily count on \panchang{} indicators. The coefficient is the log of the ratio of registrations on treated days to registrations on comparison days. Every model controls for the business day before and after a closure, when work piles up, and for the last three days of a month and of a quarter.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
\item[P1, Pitru Paksha.] Indicators for Pitru Paksha and Navratri, with fixed effects for registrar by year, registrar by week of the year, and registrar by weekday. Pitru Paksha moves about 11 days a year against the Gregorian calendar, and by about a month in years with a leap month, so the same September week falls inside it in some years and outside it in others. Predicted negative.
\item[P2, \muhurat{} days.] An indicator for any of the four named days, with fixed effects for registrar by calendar week and registrar by weekday: each named day is compared only with the other weekdays of its own week at the same registrar. Predicted positive.
\item[P3, \tithi.] A \tithi{} index equal to $+1$ on auspicious, $-1$ on inauspicious and 0 on neutral days, with the same fixed effects as P2 and controls for \nakshatra{} class and the Vishti half-day. Lunar days drift through the week, so which weekday of a given week is auspicious is set by astronomy. Predicted positive.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Inference.} The primary $p$-values come from shifting the calendar. I re-fit each model after moving the whole \panchang{} by $k$ days, for every $k$ between 20 and 300 days in either direction except shifts within two days of a whole number of lunar months, which would line the calendar up with itself again: \NShifts{} shifts. A shifted calendar keeps its own serial pattern and its relation to the week but has no link to the registry, so the shifted estimates show how large an estimate chance produces. The one-sided $p$-value is the share of shifted estimates at least as extreme as the real one, and the three are Holm-adjusted. Intervals use Driscoll--Kraay standard errors \citep{driscoll1998consistent}, which allow any correlation across registrars on a day and serial correlation over 22 business days.

\paragraph{Power.} From the shifted estimates, before estimation, the smallest effect each test detects with 80\% power at one-sided 5\% is \MdePOne\% for P1, \MdePTwo\% for P2 and \MdePThree\% per index step for P3.

\paragraph{What had been seen.} An earlier version of this project fitted composite auspiciousness indicators to 2010--2020 data and found nothing; that code dropped every Monday through a coding error and ignored holidays. Building the closure flags meant looking at daily counts, without reference to the \panchang. None of P1--P3, the named-day components, the central-registry split or the religious-name split had been estimated when the plan was committed.

\paragraph{Pitru Paksha (P1).} Registrations during Pitru Paksha are \POnePct\% different from the same weeks in years when it fell elsewhere (95\% CI \POneLo{} to \POneHi; $p = \POneP$). The interval excludes a drop larger than about a tenth, against reported drops of 40\% in car sales. Nor do registrations rebound in the Navratri that follows (\NavratriPct\%; \NavratriLo{} to \NavratriHi).

\paragraph{The lunar day (P3).} Each step of the \tithi{} index changes registrations by \PThreePct\% (\PThreeLo{} to \PThreeHi; $p = \PThreeP$). Auspicious and inauspicious days are therefore within about two percent of each other. Table~\ref{tab:components} shows the same for each component estimated freely: no class of \tithi, \nakshatra{} or \yoga{}, nor the Vishti half-day, moves registrations by more than a few percent.

\paragraph{Named days (P2).} Registrations rise \PTwoPct\% on the named days relative to the rest of their week (\PTwoLo{} to \PTwoHi; $p = \PTwoP$, Holm $p = \PTwoHolm$). Vijayadashami contributes nothing to this estimate because it is a national holiday and every registrar is closed. Table~\ref{tab:components} splits the rest: Dhanteras +\DhanterasPct\% (\DhanterasLo{} to \DhanterasHi), Gudi Padwa +\GudiPct\% (\GudiLo{} to \GudiHi) and Akshaya Tritiya \AksPct\% (\AksLo{} to \AksHi).

\section{Exploratory checks on the named days}

These checks were not in the plan; I ran them after seeing which days drove P2. Each named day sits near a calendar feature that could raise registrations without any belief. Dhanteras falls two business days before the Diwali closure (\DhanterasNearClosure\% of Dhanteras registrar-days have a closure within three business days), so founders or registrars may clear work ahead of the holiday. Gudi Padwa falls in late March in many years (\GudiNearFy\% of its registrar-days are in the last ten business days of the fiscal year), when firms rush to incorporate before 31 March. Table~\ref{tab:exploratory} adds indicators for the second and third business days before any closure and for each of the last ten business days of the fiscal year. Neither changes the estimates: Dhanteras stays at +\DhanterasBoth\% and Gudi Padwa at +\GudiBoth\%. Days before closures in general show no rise.

\section{Discussion}

The registry shows a narrow form of \panchang{} timing. A few named days that are themselves occasions for beginnings, Dhanteras and the new year, attract more registrations, possibly moved from nearby days. The broad rules of electional astrology, which classify every lunar day and mark a fortnight as unfit, leave no trace in when companies are approved.

Three features of the data limit what this establishes. The registry records approval, not filing; before March 2016 the gap between the two was long enough to blur targeting by a day, and the null for the lunar day could reflect that. The effect sizes after March 2016, when the gap was short, are similar but imprecise. The snapshot ends in January 2020, which leaves under four years of central registration. And the \muhurat{} result rests on \NDhanterasDates{} Dhanteras and \NGudiDates{} Gudi Padwa dates, and it weakens under two of six robustness checks. MCA's monthly lists of newly registered companies extend to the present and would add both precision and post-2016 years.
