\title{Do Indian Companies Register on Auspicious Days?}
\author{Gaurav Sood}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Hindu almanacs mark some days as good for starting a business and some as bad. Using all \NDomestic{} domestic companies registered in India between January 2008 and January 2020, counted by registrar and day, I test three predictions written down before estimation. During Pitru Paksha, the fortnight widely avoided for new beginnings, registrations change by \POnePct\% (95\% CI \POneLo{} to \POneHi), so a drop of more than about \POneLoAbs\% is unlikely. Auspicious and inauspicious lunar days (\tithi) differ in registrations by between \PThreeGapLo{} and \PThreeGapHi\%. Registrations rise on named \muhurat{} days by \PTwoPct\% (95\% CI \PTwoLo{} to \PTwoHi), which the pre-specified calendar-shift test rejects as chance (Holm-adjusted $p = \PTwoHolm$). The rise comes mostly from Dhanteras, the day before Diwali (+\DhanterasPct\%); Gudi Padwa (+\GudiPct\%) and Akshaya Tritiya (\AksPct\%) have intervals that include zero, and the pooled interval includes zero in three of five pre-listed robustness checks. Whether it adds registrations or moves them from neighbouring days cannot be told apart. Festival dates come from a published almanac, lunar days from the ephemeris, and registrar holidays from the government's annual holiday orders.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Indian businesses consult the \panchang, the Hindu almanac, to choose when to open a shop, sign a lease or launch a venture. Traders do \muhurat{} trading on Diwali evening, and car dealers and property registrars report steep drops during Pitru Paksha, the fortnight of rites for ancestors \citep{bs2014pitru}. If founders time company registrations the same way, the timing should be visible in the government registry, which records the date each company was incorporated.

Across \NDomestic{} registrations, the timing is small where it exists at all. Registrations during Pitru Paksha are within about \POneLoAbs\% of what the same weeks see in other years, and auspicious and inauspicious lunar days are within a few percent of each other. The one prediction that holds, and only just, is a rise of \PTwoPct\% on four named \muhurat{} days; in practice three, because Vijayadashami is a public holiday. The rise comes from one of the three: Dhanteras, the day before Diwali on which Hindus buy gold and begin ventures (+\DhanterasPct\%). Gudi Padwa, the Hindu new year in western and southern India, is +\GudiPct\% with an interval that includes zero, and Akshaya Tritiya, often called the most auspicious day of the year for new ventures, is \AksPct\% (\AksLo{} to \AksHi), which rules out the large rise its reputation would suggest.

Two features of the design matter for reading these numbers. First, the main threat is not belief but holidays. Many festivals fall on fixed lunar days and close the registrar's office, so a naive comparison finds that ``inauspicious'' days have fewer registrations because the office was shut. I take each year's holidays from the government's own holiday orders, remove every holiday on which the registrar stopped approving, and compare each day only with other days of the same week at the same registrar. Second, the three tests, their models and their power were written down and committed before any of them was run (\texttt{ms/pap.md}).

The paper adds to evidence that calendar beliefs move real decisions: births timed to the Chinese Dragon year \citep{mocan2017superstition}, prices clustered on lucky digits \citep{brown2008culture} and trading around unlucky days \citep{hirshleifer2018superstition}. Its contribution is a well-measured case in which the predicted timing, where it appears, is small enough to bound tightly.

\section{The panchang}

The \panchang{} describes each day by five astronomical quantities. The two used most for choosing days are the \tithi{} and the \nakshatra. A \tithi{} is one of 30 equal steps of the Moon's angular distance from the Sun, about a day long; a \nakshatra{} is one of 27 sectors of the sky the Moon passes through. Manuals of electional astrology classify each value as auspicious, inauspicious or neutral for beginnings \citep{kane1974history}. Table~\ref{tab:sample} gives the shares of business days in each class.

Some days are named. Gudi Padwa (also Ugadi) opens the lunar year; Akshaya Tritiya is the third lunar day of the month Vaishakha; Dhanteras falls two days before Diwali; Vijayadashami ends the autumn Navratri festival. Pitru Paksha is the waning fortnight before Navratri, given to rites for ancestors and widely avoided for purchases and new ventures.

I compute the \panchang{} for every day from 2005 to 2021 with the Swiss Ephemeris \citep{swisseph2024} at sunrise in New Delhi, following the convention that the value prevailing at sunrise names the day. Table~\ref{tab:validation} checks the computed \tithi{} and \nakshatra{} against Drik Panchang's New Delhi almanac \citep{drikpanchang2024} on random days in every year: they agree on \ValTithi{} and \ValNak{} of \NValDays{} days.

Festival dates are not computed. Each festival has a prescribed time of day (evening for Dhanteras and Diwali, afternoon for Vijayadashami and the Pitru Paksha rites, forenoon for Akshaya Tritiya), and when its lunar day touches that time on two civil days, almanacs follow different conventions; in 2024 they split over whether Diwali fell on 31 October or 1 November. I therefore take every named day and the first and last days of Pitru Paksha and Navratri from the almanac itself, for every year. My implementation of the classical rules reproduces \RuleAgree{} of the \RuleAgreeN{} almanac dates (Table~\ref{tab:validation}); the misses are a day apart, and the data use the almanac's.

\section{Data}

The Ministry of Corporate Affairs publishes a master file of companies on its register. I use the December 2020 snapshot, which lists \NCompanies{} companies registered from October 2006, when electronic filing became compulsory, to January 2020, when the snapshot ends. The analysis starts in January 2008, the first year for which the government's holiday orders could be obtained. It includes companies later struck off but may miss companies dissolved or merged before 2020; the estimates assume that whether a company survived to the snapshot is unrelated to the lunar day it was registered on. The date in the file is the date the registrar approved the company, not the date the founder applied. Until March 2016 each of \NRocs{} regional registrars approved its own companies, often days or weeks after filing; from 23 March 2016 a Central Registration Centre approved them nationally, usually within a day or two.

The unit is a registrar on a business day: \NRocDays{} registrar-days across \NBusinessDays{} weekdays. The outcome is the number of domestic non-government companies approved; foreign subsidiaries (\NForeign) and government companies (\NGovt) serve as placebo populations whose timing is set by corporate headquarters or ministries.

Closures are the main threat. Registrars close on public holidays, and many Hindu holidays fall on fixed lunar days, so a closed office would look like a day founders avoided. Registrars of Companies are central government offices, and each year the Department of Personnel and Training issues an order listing their holidays: 14 compulsory holidays everywhere, and 12 further occasions from which the committee in each state capital picks 3. I transcribed these orders for 2008--2020 from the scanned originals; every printed weekday matches its date except in two misprinted rows, neither a registrar holiday, and the transcription agrees with independent text copies of the 2013--2020 orders on every date. The states' picks were not published centrally, and the registry shows that offices did not always close on listed holidays: before March 2016 registrars stopped approving on \ShareCompulsoryShutPre\% of compulsory holidays, and the central registry afterwards on \ShareCompulsoryShutPost\%.

So the holiday orders say which days were holidays, and the registry says which of them the office actually took. I drop a listed holiday on which a registrar approved fewer than a tenth of its median for that week, or the whole country did (\NHolidayClosed{} registrar-days), and listed holidays at registrars too small to show whether they shut (\NHolidayUndetectable). Listed holidays on which the registrar worked normally (\NHolidayOpen) stay in, with an indicator in every model. I also drop \NNationalShut{} registrar-days on which the whole country shut on an unlisted day, mostly portal outages, and \NUnexplainedShut{} on which a single registrar shut on an unlisted day; some of these are state election polling days, which are paid holidays by law but are not in the holiday orders. Figure~\ref{fig:closures} shows the categories. Finally I drop \NDisruptedWeeks{} weeks in which national registrations fell below half their 17-week rolling median, chiefly April--May 2014, when the forms under the Companies Act 2013 took effect, and late March--April 2016, when the central registry took over (Figure~\ref{fig:series}). Akshaya Tritiya 2014 falls in the first of these.

\section{Design}

\paragraph{Three tests.} Each is a Poisson regression of a registrar's daily count on \panchang{} indicators. The coefficient is the log of the ratio of registrations on treated days to registrations on comparison days. Every model controls for the business day before and after a closure, when work piles up, and for the last three days of a month and of a quarter.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
\item[P1, Pitru Paksha.] Indicators for Pitru Paksha and Navratri, with fixed effects for registrar by year, registrar by week of the year, and registrar by weekday. Pitru Paksha moves about 11 days a year against the Gregorian calendar, and by about a month in years with a leap month, so the same September week falls inside it in some years and outside it in others. Predicted negative.
\item[P2, \muhurat{} days.] An indicator for any of the four named days, with fixed effects for registrar by calendar week and registrar by weekday: each named day is compared only with the other weekdays of its own week at the same registrar. Predicted positive.
\item[P3, \tithi.] A \tithi{} index equal to $+1$ on auspicious, $-1$ on inauspicious and 0 on neutral days, with the same fixed effects as P2 and controls for \nakshatra{} class and the Vishti half-day. Lunar days drift through the week, so which weekday of a given week is auspicious is set by astronomy. Predicted positive.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Inference.} The primary $p$-values come from shifting the calendar. I re-fit each model after moving the whole \panchang{} by $k$ days, for every $k$ between 20 and 300 days in either direction except shifts within two days of a whole number of lunar months, which would line the calendar up with itself again: \NShifts{} shifts. A shifted calendar keeps its own serial pattern and its relation to the week but has no link to the registry, so the shifted estimates show how large an estimate chance produces. The one-sided $p$-value is the share of shifted estimates at least as extreme as the real one, and the three are Holm-adjusted. Intervals use Driscoll--Kraay standard errors \citep{driscoll1998consistent}, which allow any correlation across registrars on a day and serial correlation over 22 business days.

\paragraph{Power.} From the shifted estimates, before estimation, the smallest effect each test detects with 80\% power at one-sided 5\% is \MdePOne\% for P1, \MdePTwo\% for P2 and \MdePThree\% per index step for P3.

\paragraph{What had been seen.} An earlier version of this project fitted composite auspiciousness indicators to 2010--2020 data and found small, imprecise estimates; that code dropped every Monday through a coding error and ignored holidays. Building the closure flags meant looking at daily counts, without reference to the \panchang. None of P1--P3, the named-day components, the central-registry split or the religious-name split had been estimated when the plan was committed. After the first estimates I replaced the two weakest data sources: festival dates computed from my own rules became the almanac's, and holidays from a software package became the government's holiday orders, which also moved the start of the sample from October 2006 to January 2008. The models and tests did not change; the plan records the revision.

\paragraph{Pitru Paksha (P1).} Registrations during Pitru Paksha change by \POnePct\% relative to the same weeks in years when it fell elsewhere (95\% CI \POneLo{} to \POneHi; $p = \POneP$). The interval excludes a drop larger than about \POneLoAbs\%, against reported drops of 40\% in car sales. In the Navratri that follows they change by \NavratriPct\% (\NavratriLo{} to \NavratriHi), so a rebound of more than about \NavratriHi\% is unlikely.

\paragraph{The lunar day (P3).} Each step of the \tithi{} index changes registrations by \PThreePct\% (\PThreeLo{} to \PThreeHi; $p = \PThreeP$). Auspicious and inauspicious days, two steps apart on the index, are therefore within about three percent of each other. Table~\ref{tab:components} shows the same for each component estimated freely: no class of \tithi, \nakshatra{} or \yoga{}, nor the Vishti half-day, moves registrations by more than a few percent.

\paragraph{Named days (P2).} Registrations rise \PTwoPct\% on the named days relative to the rest of their week. The calendar-shift test, the pre-specified inference, puts the estimate beyond nearly all shifted estimates ($p = \PTwoP$, Holm $p = \PTwoHolm$), but the Driscoll--Kraay interval runs from \PTwoLo{} to \PTwoHi{} and includes zero; the two methods disagree at the margin. Vijayadashami does not enter this estimate because it is a national holiday and every registrar is closed. Table~\ref{tab:components} splits the rest: Dhanteras +\DhanterasPct\% (\DhanterasLo{} to \DhanterasHi), Gudi Padwa +\GudiPct\% (\GudiLo{} to \GudiHi) and Akshaya Tritiya \AksPct\% (\AksLo{} to \AksHi).

\section{Exploratory checks on the named days}

These checks were not in the plan; I ran them after seeing which days drove P2. Each named day sits near a calendar feature that could raise registrations without any belief. Dhanteras falls two business days before the Diwali closure (\DhanterasNearClosure\% of Dhanteras registrar-days have a closure within three business days), so founders or registrars may clear work ahead of the holiday. Gudi Padwa falls in late March in many years (\GudiNearFy\% of its registrar-days are in the last ten business days of the fiscal year), when firms rush to incorporate before 31 March. Table~\ref{tab:exploratory} adds indicators for the second and third business days before any closure and for each of the last ten business days of the fiscal year. Neither changes the estimates: Dhanteras stays at +\DhanterasBoth\% and Gudi Padwa at +\GudiBoth\%. Registrations on the second and third business days before a closure change by between \AheadLo{} and \AheadHi\%, far less than the Dhanteras rise, so the spike is not a general pre-holiday rush.

\section{Discussion}

The registry shows a narrow form of \panchang{} timing. One named day that is itself an occasion for beginnings, Dhanteras, attracts more registrations, possibly moved from nearby days; the evidence for the other named days is weak or absent. The broad rules of electional astrology, which classify every lunar day and mark a fortnight as unfit, move registrations little: auspicious and inauspicious lunar days are within a few percent of each other, and Pitru Paksha is within about \POneLoAbs\% of ordinary weeks.

Three features of the data limit what this establishes. The registry records approval, not filing; before March 2016 the gap between the two was long enough to blur targeting by a day, and the narrow interval for the lunar day could reflect that. The effect sizes after March 2016, when the gap was short, are similar but imprecise. The snapshot ends in January 2020, which leaves under four years of central registration. And the \muhurat{} result rests on \NDhanterasDates{} Dhanteras dates, and its interval includes zero in three of five robustness checks. MCA's monthly lists of newly registered companies extend to the present and would add both precision and post-2016 years.
